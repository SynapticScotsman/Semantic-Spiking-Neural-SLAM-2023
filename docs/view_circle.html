Object-centric VSA cognitive map · view-direction read-out

Where the view circle holds, and where it breaks

An object file stores what a thing looks like from each side. Show it a crop and it returns a likelihood over the whole circle of viewpoints — the peak is the side you are looking from. This page is the instrument: pick an object, scrub the orbit, and watch the likelihood succeed or fail. Two failure modes, and they need opposite fixes.

Provenance — synthetic illustration

Every number here is measured on a procedurally rendered turntable with a HOG descriptor, on a blocked split with 30° held-out arcs. It is not a measurement of any real encoder, dataset or robot, and must not be reported as one. The algebra underneath (exact 2π periodicity, binding as rotation, the one-FFT likelihood) is exact and separately verified to 1e-16.

It fails in two different ways

Lumping both into one error number hides the important distinction: one is a property of the object that no amount of extra data will fix, the other is a coverage problem that more stored sides solves directly.

aliasing

The likelihood has several equal peaks

The object genuinely looks the same from more than one side, so the peak height carries no information about which one you are at. The estimator is not wrong — the question has more than one right answer. Watch the cube in the dial above: four-fold symmetry, four peaks.

single peak

One peak, and the error is the distance to it

Where the likelihood is unimodal, what remains is how far the nearest stored side is. The code interpolates between sides it holds and does not extrapolate past them, so error here is a coverage number, not a representational one.

— median error at unimodal azimuths (— of 216)

Both figures are computed in the page from the same data driving the dial, so they cannot drift from what you see above. A peak counts as competing when it reaches 80% of the maximum and sits more than two kernel widths away.

It cannot teleport — adding continuity

Decoded frame by frame, the estimate jumps 50°, 100°, 180° between consecutive crops. A camera orbiting an object cannot do that, and every one of those jumps is the decoder throwing away the strongest prior it has: you were near here a moment ago. Feeding the likelihood into a Bayes filter on the circle fixes it, and both steps of the filter are native to this representation.

the filter, in the same algebra
stepon the circlein harmonics
predictshift the belief by the odometry step, then blur itB_k ← B_k · e−ikΔ · e−σ²k²/2
updatemultiply by the appearance likelihoodb ← b · softmaxβ(L)

The first factor in the predict step is binding by S_view(Δ) — the same operation as orbit(). The second is per-harmonic damping. Nothing outside the representation was needed. This is the view-circle twin of the position filter in RESULTS_SO_FAR.md finding 6.

Four equal branches give the filter nothing to choose between, so tracking alone wanders. But continuity turns a per-frame ambiguity into a single global one: fix the branch once, at the start, and it stays fixed. Symmetry costs one piece of information for the whole trajectory, not one per frame.

Two different things are called anisotropy

One of them is the encoder’s fault and one of them is not, and they need opposite responses. All three numbers below come from the crops alone — no object file, no split, no decode.

removable

Between-object

The leading principal directions say which object this is — PC0 carries 83.8% between-object variance, PC1 70.5%. These are what the conditioning recipe deletes.

not the encoder

Across-view

Seven views spanning 30° of orbit occupy about 3.8 effective dimensions out of 1764 — and that number does not move when you condition. It is what walking a continuous trajectory produces, for any encoder. The same thing as scene redundancy in a video.

They are linked, but backwards from the obvious guess

An object’s identity does not change as you walk around it. So the identity-carrying directions are constant along the orbit, and sit as a DC pedestal on the appearance autocorrelation, lifting every lag equally. Deleting them does not create angular information — it unmasks what was already there.

The cube stands alone at 0.910, peaking exactly at its 90° symmetry. One autocorrelation per object tells you which instances will be unlocalisable and at what offset — which is precisely the information the continuity filter needs an anchor for.

It has to be circular distance. On a 72-view ring, a lag of 355° is the adjacent frame. Scanning linear lag makes five of six objects report their own neighbour as an alias. That bug was in the first version of this analysis and inverted the ranking.

The DINOv2 row is missing, and stays missing

Everything on this page runs on HOG. The obvious next column is DINOv2, and it is not here because this environment's egress policy refuses huggingface.co, dl.fbaipublicfiles.com and download.pytorch.org — all three return 403 before TLS. There is no honest way to fill that column from here, so it is left empty rather than substituted.

The wiring exists and is one flag on a machine that can reach the weights:

python experiments/run_view_localisation.py --encoder dinov2 --plot

What to expect to change: DINOv2's spectrum is far flatter than HOG's — 49 of 256 directions carry half the variance against about 3 here — so the latent-conditioning depth will not transfer. The two failure modes should, since aliasing is a property of the object and coverage is a property of the orbit, and neither depends on the encoder. High-norm outlier patch tokens are the one DINOv2-specific hazard, and the --encoder dinov2 path already drops tokens above twice the median norm before pooling.

A hypothesis this page refutes

The obvious explanation was that localisation fails where appearance stops changing with angle — a flat stretch of the orbit gives nothing to key on. It is wrong. The correlation between local appearance rate |dc/dφ| and error is positive (ρ = +0.38): appearance changing faster goes with worse localisation, the opposite of the prediction.

Correction — an earlier version of this page quoted “n = 216, p = 7.6e-9”

That n counted frames. Consecutive views on an orbit are strongly correlated, so they are not independent samples: integrating the appearance autocorrelation gives about 6 independent views per 72-frame orbit, making the effective n nearer 19 than 216. The p-value was therefore meaningless and has been withdrawn. The sign and the ranking below stand — ρ is positive under every leave-one-object-out exclusion, and the aliasing split is a difference between two large groups rather than a correlation. Any interval computed by resampling frames needs a hierarchical bootstrap over objects, arcs and seeds instead.

Applying the breadth check from RESULTS_SO_FAR.md — does the effect survive removing its strongest object? — it does survive, but weakly, and the sign never flips:

The cube halves it, which is the tell: the cube has both the fastest appearance change (sharp faces flipping) and the worst error (it aliases). Rate was a proxy for symmetry, not a cause. Restricted to unimodal azimuths only, ρ falls to +0.202 and the gradient is nearly flat — median error 21.8° → 24.5° → 26.8° across the full range of appearance rate. Peak multiplicity explains the failures; appearance rate barely does.

It interpolates. It does not extrapolate.

Widening the held-out arc moves the nearest kept side further away. Error inside the gap tracks that distance almost linearly up to chance, while the control — the same object file read at sides it kept — never moves. The file is not degraded by the harder split; the missing viewpoints simply are not recoverable.

What each failure asks for

for aliasing

Break the symmetry or report the ambiguity

  • More stored sides do nothing — the peaks stay equal.
  • Return the whole likelihood, not the argmax. ViewLocalisation.margin already measures the gap to the runner-up peak, and it is small exactly here.
  • Fuse with odometry rather than trusting the peak; a flat multi-peak likelihood is still informative as a likelihood.
  • Break the tie with a cue the pooled descriptor discards — a part at a known surface location is the natural candidate.
for coverage

Store sides at the kernel's spacing

  • To localise better than 15°, sides must sit no further apart than the view kernel's half-width — 16° at max_harmonic=8.
  • That is ~22 sides for a full circle, not the "a few diverse sides" the first write-up recommended. That advice was wrong.
  • merge_tol should be derived from lobe_width(), not set by hand.
  • Widening the kernel (lower max_harmonic) buys coverage at the cost of angular resolution — it is the same trade as an SSP length scale.
