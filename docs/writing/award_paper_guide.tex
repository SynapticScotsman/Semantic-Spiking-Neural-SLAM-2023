\begin{abstract}
\noindent What ICRA, RSS, CoRL and T-RO award winners actually do, as
opposed to what paper-writing advice usually says they do: section budgets,
the narrative arc, title and abstract construction, and the style rules that
separate winners from competent-but-unremarkable submissions. Every claim is
tagged by how well the evidence supports it, and the closing section lists
the reasons not to trust it too far. Use it as a revision checklist against
a draft you have already written, not as a template to write into.
\end{abstract}

\section{How this was built, and how to read it}

Eighteen agents ran four parallel sweeps --- ICRA award winners, RSS/CoRL/T-RO
award winners, the craft of openings, and what award citations and review
criteria actually reward --- fetching and reading the papers themselves rather
than their abstracts or secondary summaries. Forty-seven papers were surveyed;
fourteen were read in full. Load-bearing claims were spot-verified against
primary sources, and an adversarial critic checked the evidence base for
attribution errors, sample bias and survivorship bias. The critic's findings
are in Section~\ref{sec:caveats} and are not decoration: it caught the sweep's
own fact-checker mis-refuting a correct claim.

Three tags appear throughout, and they mean different strengths of evidence:

\begin{itemize}
  \item[] \vtag{verified} --- checked directly against a primary source
    (a call for papers, an official award page).
  \item[] \atag{attested} --- observed in named papers that were actually
    read, and traceable to them.
  \item[] \jtag{judgment} --- an inference from the pattern rather than a
    directly evidenced claim. Weigh accordingly.
\end{itemize}

A fourth category matters just as much: papers recorded as
\emph{listing-only} were \textbf{not read}. Their rows in the reference table
establish only that an award exists, and support no structural claim. Any
frequency statement below (``seven of eight winners\ldots'') counts only the
papers that were read.

\section{Venue rules that shape everything else}

\vtag{verified against the ICRA 2027 call for papers, 2026-08-18} These are
venue-specific and dated; re-check them before every submission cycle. They
are listed first because several otherwise-excellent award-paper habits are
simply illegal under them.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Eight pages including references}, with no purchasable extra
    pages. References are not free, so a long bibliography is a real
    structural cost.
  \item \textbf{Double-anonymous review.} Author names, affiliations,
    identifying acknowledgements and grant numbers all come out. This kills
    one habit common among winners: project and code URLs placed in the
    Figure~1 caption.
  \item \textbf{No supplementary PDF.} A video is permitted (20\,MB,
    180\,s). Nothing load-bearing may live outside the page limit --- so the
    ``exile the derivation to an appendix'' move, which CoRL papers use
    freely and which some ICRA winners used in their arXiv versions, is not
    available.
  \item \textbf{AI-tool use must be disclosed} in the acknowledgements, and
    generative tools cannot be listed as authors.
  \item Papers carrying a DOI (for example an RA-L version) are ineligible;
    preprints are fine.
\end{itemize}

\noindent The general lesson survives the specifics: \emph{read the current
CFP before taking structural advice from any published paper}, because the
paper you are imitating was written under different rules.

\section{The skeleton}

Budgets below assume eight two-column pages --- sixteen columns --- with
roughly 1.25 columns of references and 4.5 columns of floats, leaving about
ten columns of body text. Treat them as proportions rather than targets:
they were estimated from arXiv HTML, and arXiv versions are routinely longer
than the camera-ready they came from.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lcX@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Section} & \textbf{Cols} & \textbf{What winners do here} \\
\midrule
Title + abstract & 0.5 &
  Five to eight sentences. The contribution sentence arrives no later than
  sentence four. One headline number early; the rest are relocated to the
  Figure~1 caption. \\
\addlinespace
Hero figure & $\sim$1 &
  A teaser \emph{result} drawn from the system's own state --- not an
  architecture block diagram. Caption of 40--85 words that pre-empts the
  most likely objection inline. \\
\addlinespace
I. Introduction & 1.25 &
  Three to six paragraphs closing on at most four refutable contribution
  bullets with forward section references. The specific gap is reached
  within three or four sentences. \\
\addlinespace
II. Related work & 0.75 &
  Deliberately thin, and sometimes absent entirely. One contrast per
  paragraph, each naming the alternative it displaces. \\
\addlinespace
III. Method & 2.5 &
  The largest block, but every subsection must be a stage of \emph{one}
  mechanism. Background teaches only the tools the method actually uses. \\
\addlinespace
IV. Theory / analysis & 1.75 &
  One formal statement in the main text, placed where the objection lives;
  derivations deferred. Optional --- but when present, it is what the award
  citation tends to name. \\
\addlinespace
V. Experiments & 3 &
  A claims ledger: hypotheses or questions declared up front, tagged into
  subsection titles, and closed explicitly. Setup folds into bold run-in
  heads rather than occupying its own section. \\
\addlinespace
VI. Conclusion \& discussion & 0.5 &
  Limitations merged in, carrying numbers, phrased as scope with a path
  forward. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\noindent Two structural findings are worth stating plainly because they
contradict common advice. \atag{attested} First, \textbf{a standalone
Limitations section is atypical among winners} --- seven of the eight ICRA
winners read have none, and the exception merges it into a combined
``Discussion and Limitations'' heading. Honesty is not the casualty:
limitations still appear, quantified, inside the discussion. Second,
\textbf{Related Work is a trim target, not a proving ground}. One paper that
won both an overall and a category award at ICRA spends under a fifth of its
body text there, and one overall winner has no Related Work section at all.

\section{The narrative spine}

The arc that recurs across winners, in general form. Each move is one or two
sentences in the abstract, then expanded in the introduction.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\textbf{\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Name the enemy.} A concrete failure mode of the current
    approach --- growth, silent degradation, brittleness --- stated as a
    property, not a statistic. \atag{attested} No sampled winner opens on a
    statistic.
  \item \textbf{The turn: why the obvious fix fails.} Credit the obvious
    alternative, then name the specific mechanism by which it breaks. This
    is where a citation to a survey or a competing system does the most work.
  \item \textbf{``We present X'', with the mechanism inside the sentence.}
    Stated as a contrast --- ``instead of X, we do Y'' --- so the idea
    survives being read once.
  \item \textbf{The quotable claim.} One sentence a reader could repeat at a
    poster session. Papers that coin a handle for their own idea get cited
    with that handle.
  \item \textbf{Validation scope, with the steelman visible.} Name the
    baselines \emph{and} say how you strengthened them: matched operating
    points, ground-truth parameters handed to the competitor, a published
    result reproduced before it is beaten.
  \item \textbf{The payoff, including the honest loss.} Where the method
    loses, and why that is consistent with the thesis.
  \item \textbf{Coin the thesis and close.} One phrase, repeated verbatim
    wherever the thesis appears.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Coin one phrase, then never paraphrase it}

\atag{attested} Winners hand the community a citable handle and use exactly
those words every time --- in the title where possible, then the abstract,
the section that establishes it, and the conclusion. Drafts commonly carry
three near-synonymous phrasings of their own thesis, which is three chances
for the reader to fail to remember any of them. Pick the strongest and
propagate it.

\section{Openings: title, abstract, first figure}

\subsection{Title}

\atag{attested} The median award title in the sample is \textbf{eight
words}. Two constructions dominate: a pronounceable system name followed by
a colon and the mechanism; or a territory-grab claim with no colon at all,
naming a problem or asserting a capability. A third, rarer and effective,
states the finding itself as a sentence with an active verb.

\noindent Zero sampled winners use ``Towards'', ``A Novel'' or ``A Framework
for''. None carries a numeral. Titles that enumerate two or three claims are
a median-submission habit: winners encode one.

\subsection{Abstract}

A seven-sentence skeleton, one role per sentence: \emph{(1)} the enemy;
\emph{(2)} the gap, ideally in the field's own words via a cited survey;
\emph{(3)} ``we present'', mechanism included, plus the object's identity
number if it has one; \emph{(4)} the central claim with its headline number;
\emph{(5)} validation scope with the steelman clause; \emph{(6)} the result,
including where it loses; \emph{(7)} the coined thesis.

\atag{attested} \textbf{Numbers policy is where most drafts leak.} Award
abstracts carry \emph{one} headline number early --- one paper puts its
single figure in sentence two, before any mechanism detail --- and relocate
identity and scale numbers to the Figure~1 caption. A draft with five or
more metrics scattered through the abstract is displaying median-submission
behaviour. Two winners in the sample carry \emph{no} numbers at all, trading
them for one crisp capability claim; this works only when the claim is an
unlock rather than an improvement.

Counts are an underused move: ``in all nine conditions tested'' asserts
coverage in a way a mean cannot, and is harder to argue with.

\subsection{The first figure}

\atag{attested} The contract, in order of how often it is violated:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It shows a result, not an architecture.} The strongest
    examples show failure-then-fix, or the system's output drawn from its own
    state. Only a consortium-scale dataset paper in the sample gets away with
    a plain block diagram.
  \item \textbf{The caption is a mini-abstract of 40--85 words}, in the
    present tense, whose first clause restates the paper's claim rather than
    describing the panels.
  \item \textbf{It carries numbers}, normalised into units a reader feels ---
    a distance as a multiple of the robot's own height, a memory footprint as
    a ratio against the baseline it replaces.
  \item \textbf{It pre-empts the top objection in-line.} A single defensive
    clause in the caption, answering the thing a referee would otherwise
    write in the margin, is one of the cheapest credibility moves available.
  \item Under single-blind review, winners also put the project or code URL
    here. \vtag{not available under double-anonymous review.}
\end{itemize}

\section{Style rules}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\textbf{S\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{One idea; everything else is a witness of it.}
    \atag{attested} Winners show one derivation instantiated several ways
    rather than several loosely-related results. Generality is
    \emph{demonstrated structurally} --- the same result dropped into three
    settings --- not asserted in the introduction.
  \item \textbf{Every claim carries its evidence unit inline.} A number, a
    named baseline, a named condition, and a section reference. Never a bare
    percentage; never ``better'' without ``than what''.
  \item \textbf{Freeze numbers verbatim.} The same figure appears
    identically in abstract, introduction and tables --- no rounding drift,
    no ``approximately'' in one place and three significant figures in
    another.
  \item \textbf{Denominate claims in the constrained resource.} When a paper
    is about a budget, every result is priced in that budget's units and
    compared against a hard limit, and the method's own ceiling is stated as
    a first-class number rather than hidden.
  \item \textbf{Report errors against floors and exchange rates.} An error
    is meaningless without the physical floor it approaches or the cost it
    was traded for. A worse accuracy at a much lower cost should never appear
    without the cost in the same sentence.
  \item \textbf{Steelman the baselines, then say that you did.} Re-tune the
    competitor's threshold, hand it privileged information, reproduce its
    published result before comparing. \atag{attested} This is the single
    clearest cultural marker of award-level work, and it is worth a sentence
    of its own rather than being buried in setup.
  \item \textbf{Attribute failures with counts and mechanisms.} ``$k$ of $n$
    failures were caused by \emph{named mechanism}'' converts a weakness into
    evidence of understanding.
  \item \textbf{Hedge mechanism and universality; never hedge measurements.}
    State what was measured flatly. Mark speculation explicitly. Show
    distributions, not only means. Frame generality as a falsifiable
    forecast.
  \item \textbf{Answer the reviewer in the text.} A bold run-in question
    (``Has performance saturated?'') costs two lines and defuses an
    objection that would otherwise cost a score.
  \item \textbf{Voice: measured, zero superlatives.} \atag{attested} Award
    citations reward rigour, generality, practicality and convincing results
    on hard benchmarks. They do not mention novelty or state-of-the-art
    margins. Write the paper the citation would describe.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Anti-patterns}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Title padding: ``Towards'', ``A Novel'', ``A Framework for'', or
    three claims where one belongs.
  \item Five or more metrics scattered through the abstract.
  \item Closing an abstract with ``experiments demonstrate the effectiveness
    of the proposed method''. No sampled winner does this.
  \item Terse figure captions (``System overview.'').
  \item A bare architecture diagram as Figure~1.
  \item Multiple vague ``first'' claims. Winners make at most one, tightly
    scoped.
  \item Mechanism-free verbs in claims: \emph{provides}, \emph{enables},
    \emph{allows}.
  \item A long early Related Work section explaining trade-offs the reader
    has no context for yet.
  \item Hiding losses. \atag{attested} A best-paper winner in the sample
    prints a middling success rate and calls its own system impractical for
    industry --- and won, because the claim it defended was a representational
    bridge, not deployment readiness. A loss with a one-sentence mechanistic
    diagnosis becomes a demonstration of the thesis; a hidden loss becomes a
    rebuttal.
  \item Anything load-bearing in supplementary material.
\end{itemize}

\section{Revision checklist}

Run this against a draft that already exists. Roughly in order of value per
minute spent.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \ck \textbf{Abstract}: seven sentences; contribution by sentence four; one
    headline number; the central result present (drafts routinely omit their
    own best evidence while keeping minor numbers); ends on the coined
    thesis.
  \ck \textbf{Title}: one claim, under about eleven words, no banned openers,
    no numerals.
  \ck \textbf{Thesis phrase}: identical wording at every station where it
    appears.
  \ck \textbf{Numbers audit}: every headline figure identical in abstract,
    introduction, tables and conclusion.
  \ck \textbf{Claims}: each has a number, a named baseline, a named
    condition, and a section reference.
  \ck \textbf{Figure~1}: shows a result; caption 40--85 words; opens with the
    claim; carries normalised numbers; pre-empts one objection.
  \ck \textbf{Contributions}: at most four bullets, each refutable, each with
    a forward reference.
  \ck \textbf{Limitations}: merged into the discussion, quantified, phrased
    as scope with a path.
  \ck \textbf{Related Work}: under about 0.75 columns; one contrast per
    paragraph.
  \ck \textbf{Steelmanning}: stated explicitly in its own sentence.
  \ck \textbf{Losses}: present in the main tables, each with a one-sentence
    mechanistic diagnosis.
  \ck \textbf{Compliance}: page limit including references; anonymisation;
    AI-use disclosure; nothing load-bearing outside the limit.
\end{itemize}

\section{Caveats}
\label{sec:caveats}

Reasons not to over-trust the above, in rough order of seriousness.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Survivorship bias is unaddressed.} Every pattern here is
    conditioned on winning. Without a matched sample of rejected or
    median-accepted papers, this cannot distinguish features that
    \emph{cause} awards from features common to all competent papers --- or
    from lab-brand confounds, where a paper may win despite its structure
    rather than because of it.
  \item \textbf{Awards and acceptance are different games.} Awards are
    decided by a small committee \emph{after} acceptance; the binding gate
    for a submission is a handful of anonymous reviewers. Award-optimal
    boldness and acceptance-optimal defensiveness are correlated but not
    identical, and only the former is observable here.
  \item \textbf{The sample skews} toward learning and perception systems
    papers from a small number of well-known labs. Papers in
    theory-and-representation genres, and the reviewers those genres draw,
    are underrepresented.
  \item \textbf{Roughly half the corpus is RSS, CoRL or T-RO.} Several of
    their signature moves --- hypothesis ledgers, related work after results,
    long appendices --- have no verified instance among the ICRA winners
    read. Treat them as imported, and remember appendices may be unavailable.
  \item \textbf{Column budgets are estimates} from arXiv HTML, which is
    frequently an extended version of what was actually submitted.
  \item \textbf{The survey's own fact-checking layer was fallible.} It
    ``refuted'' a correct claim about one year's winners by consulting a page
    that listed only the category awards. Two papers analysed as exemplars
    are influential but not verified award winners. Verify any award
    attribution before repeating it.
\end{itemize}
